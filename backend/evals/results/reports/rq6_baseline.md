# What are the environmental costs of training and deploying large language models? (baseline)

The rapid proliferation of large language models (LLMs) has sparked significant concern regarding their environmental footprint, primarily driven by energy consumption during training and inference. Training a single large-scale model involves thousands of GPUs running for weeks, resulting in substantial carbon emissions. Research indicates that the training of GPT-3, for instance, consumed approximately 1,287 MWh of electricity and emitted over 500 tonnes of CO2 equivalent, comparable to the lifetime emissions of five average cars [1]. This carbon intensity is exacerbated by the hardware manufacturing process, which accounts for a significant portion of the total lifecycle emissions [2]. Beyond training, the deployment phase—often overlooked—contributes heavily to the total environmental cost. Inference, the process of generating text in response to user queries, requires continuous computational resources. Studies suggest that for models like GPT-3, inference can account for a substantial fraction of the total energy footprint over the model's lifespan, particularly as the number of user requests scales [3]. The environmental impact is not uniform; it is highly dependent on the carbon intensity of the energy grid where the data centers are located [4]. Researchers have proposed various mitigation strategies, including the use of sparse models, model distillation, and optimizing training schedules to utilize renewable energy sources during off-peak hours [5]. Furthermore, there is a growing call for transparency in reporting the carbon footprint of AI models, with frameworks developed to standardize the measurement of energy consumption and carbon emissions across the machine learning lifecycle [6]. However, the 'rebound effect' remains a challenge, where increased efficiency in model training leads to more widespread deployment, potentially offsetting the gains made by optimization techniques [7]. Ultimately, balancing the transformative potential of LLMs with sustainable practices requires a holistic approach that considers hardware efficiency, energy sourcing, and the necessity of model size for specific tasks [8].
