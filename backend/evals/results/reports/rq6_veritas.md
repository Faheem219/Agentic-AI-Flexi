# What are the environmental costs of training and deploying large language models?

## Executive Summary
The rapid expansion of large language models (LLMs) has introduced significant environmental challenges, spanning the entire lifecycle from hardware manufacturing to continuous operational deployment [1][2]. While training remains a high-intensity, concentrated burst of energy consumption, inference and deployment often dominate the total environmental footprint due to their cumulative, long-term nature [3][4]. Addressing these impacts requires a holistic approach that accounts for embodied carbon in hardware, energy-efficient model architectures, and standardized carbon accounting frameworks [5][6][7].

## Key Findings

### Energy Consumption and Carbon Emissions in Model Training
Training foundational LLMs is a computationally demanding process that consumes substantial electricity, with total energy usage derived from a combination of raw computation, infrastructure overhead, and datacenter networking [1][8][9]. Researchers have developed reproducible frameworks that utilize empirically derived FLOP-to-MWh conversion factors and region-specific grid carbon intensity data to estimate the CO2-equivalent emissions of these training runs [10]. While these estimations provide a baseline, the environmental cost is highly sensitive to training configurations, including numerical precision, data parallelization strategies, and the underlying datacenter network architecture [7][11][12]. Optimizing these variables—such as employing rail-only network designs—has been shown to significantly reduce the power requirements of large-scale training [7].

### The Cumulative Impact of Model Inference and Deployment
While training captures significant public attention, the inference phase often accounts for a larger share of the total lifecycle environmental impact due to the continuous, large-scale demand for model services [3][4]. Simulation frameworks suggest that for many deployments, inference constitutes more than half of total lifecycle carbon emissions [4]. This footprint is heavily influenced by model size and technical choices made during deployment, such as quantization and distillation, which trade off translation accuracy for reduced energy consumption [13]. Furthermore, inefficient prompt engineering techniques—which may necessitate multiple inferences or larger context windows—can lead to disproportionate increases in electrical usage when scaled across millions of users [14]. When LLMs are deployed on edge or mobile devices, the environmental burden shifts toward battery life, hardware performance loads, and accelerated device replacement cycles [15].

### Embodied Carbon: Hardware Lifecycle and Manufacturing Costs
The environmental cost of AI extends beyond operational electricity to include the "embodied carbon" generated during the manufacturing, mining, and disposal of specialized hardware like GPUs [2]. Although often overlooked in standard carbon accounting, embodied carbon represents a measurable percentage of the total footprint, accounting for approximately 0.77% of GPT-3’s and 2.18% of GPT-4’s reported operational emissions [16]. While the operational phase dominates climate change and fossil fuel depletion metrics during training, hardware manufacturing dominates other critical environmental categories, such as human toxicity and resource depletion [17]. Current sustainability frameworks frequently treat embodied carbon too simplistically, failing to capture the full cradle-to-grave impact of AI infrastructure [5][6].

## Debates & Limitations
A primary debate in the field concerns the lack of standardized metrics for reporting environmental costs across different providers [3][4]. Because many frontier model architectures and their associated training runs are proprietary, researchers struggle to obtain precise, verified data on global energy consumption [3][4]. Furthermore, there is a tension between the need for model accuracy and the environmental imperative for efficiency; while quantization and distillation reduce carbon footprints, they may impact the quality of model outputs [13]. Additionally, current accounting practices are criticized for focusing almost exclusively on carbon emissions while failing to provide a comprehensive view of other environmental stressors, such as water usage and electronic waste [2][5].

## Research Gaps & Future Directions
Significant gaps remain in our understanding of the long-term environmental impact of AI systems [16][17]. There is a scarcity of empirical, cradle-to-grave quantitative data for newer LLM architectures beyond the well-studied GPT series, making it difficult to assess how advancements in model efficiency are keeping pace with increased model scale [16][17]. Furthermore, the industry lacks detailed data on the environmental impacts of electronic waste disposal and the efficiency of recycling specialized AI accelerators at scale [16]. Future research should prioritize the development of standardized, transparent carbon-accounting frameworks that incorporate market-based attribution and account for the full hardware lifecycle [6].

## Conclusion: Balancing Innovation with Environmental Responsibility
The environmental footprint of LLMs is a multifaceted issue that necessitates a shift from focusing solely on training-phase energy to addressing the entire lifecycle of AI systems [1][9]. While technical optimizations in model architecture and hardware design offer promising pathways for mitigation, these efforts must be supported by transparent reporting and standardized metrics [10][6]. Achieving a balance between the rapid pace of AI innovation and environmental responsibility will require industry-wide cooperation to minimize the cumulative impact of inference and address the often-hidden costs of hardware manufacturing [2][3].
